
# **Example 1:** Retail Industry – Safeway's Olive Oil Promotion

## 1. Background and business problem

In April 2026, the Washington State Attorney General filed a lawsuit against Albertsons Companies, which owns Safeway, alleging that some of its **stores artificially increased product prices before advertising** Buy One Get One Free (BOGO) promotions.

One example in the lawsuit involved a bottle of olive oil at an Albertsons store in Gig Harbor, Washington.

According to the Attorney General's allegations:
- The original price of olive oil was $6.99.
- The price increased to $10.99 before or during a BOGO promotion.
- After the promotion ended, the price returned to $6.99.

This represented an increase of approximately 57%.

The allegation was that customers were led to believe they were receiving a genuine bargain when the actual savings were questionable.

**2. Concrete decision supported by data analysis**

**Decision**: Should Safeway change its pricing and promotional strategy to ensure that BOGO promotions offer genuine savings to customers?

**Data analysis could help Safeway investigate historical product prices and identify whether prices were increased shortly before promotional campaigns.**

For example, Safeway's management could introduce a pricing policy requiring promotional prices to be compared against the product's actual historical selling price.

If the analysis identifies unusual price increases immediately before promotions, management could investigate them and prevent misleading promotional pricing.

**3. Data required**

To make this decision, a data analyst would need the following information:

| Data required | Why it is needed |
| --- | --- |
| Historical product prices | To compare prices before, during and after promotions. |
| Promotion dates | To identify when BOGO offers started and ended. |
| Product information | To identify the specific olive oil products and sizes. |
| Sales transaction records | To determine how much customers actually paid. |
| Quantity sold | To measure customer purchasing behaviour during promotions. |
| Customer complaints | To identify concerns about misleading discounts. |
| Product costs | To understand the actual profit margins. |

The analyst could compare prices over a period of 60–90 days before and after each promotion.

**4. Metric to measure whether the decision worked**

The main metric would be:

Genuine Customer Savings (%)

This measures whether customers actually receive a meaningful discount compared with the normal selling price before the promotion.

For example:

| Description | Price |
| --- | --- |
| Normal historical price | $6.99 |
| Price during promotion | $10.99 |
| Price after promotion | $6.99 |

The analyst would investigate whether the promotional offer genuinely provides savings compared with the historical price.

Other useful metrics include:
- Percentage of promotions with genuine discounts.
- Number of customer complaints about promotional pricing.
- Average selling price before and during promotions.
- Customer satisfaction levels.

How would we know the decision worked?

The decision would be successful if Safeway's promotions consistently offered genuine savings, customers experienced fewer misleading pricing practices, and complaints about promotional pricing decreased.

**5. Conclusion**

Data analysis helps retailers make informed pricing decisions while maintaining customer trust. In the Safeway example, analyzing historical prices, promotional periods and customer transactions could help identify questionable pricing patterns.

Rather than focusing only on increasing sales and profits, retailers should also consider whether their pricing **strategies are fair and transparent to customers**.

Connecting this example to the data analytics process

| Analytics stage | Application to Safeway |
| --- | --- |
| Ask | Are Safeway's BOGO promotions offering genuine customer savings? |
| Obtain | Collect historical prices, sales transactions and promotional records. |
| Scrub | Remove duplicate records and correct missing or inconsistent prices. |
| Explore | Compare prices before, during and after promotions. |
| Model | Identify unusual price increases associated with promotional periods. |
| Interpret/Communicate | Present findings and recommend fairer pricing policies. |

# **Example 2: Healthcare Industry – Reducing Patient Waiting Times**

## 1. Concrete decision supported by data analysis

A hospital wants to reduce the amount of time patients spend waiting before seeing a doctor. The hospital can use **data analysis to decide whether it should employ more doctors during busy periods or adjust staff schedules to match patient demand**. For example, if data shows that Mondays and Tuesdays have the highest number of patients and the longest waiting times, management could schedule more doctors and nurses on those days.

## 2. Data required

The hospital would need to collect the following data:

- Patient registration dates and times.

- Patient arrival and appointment times.

- Time patients are attended to by doctors.

- Number of patients visiting the hospital each day.

- Number of doctors and nurses working per shift.

- Patient feedback or satisfaction ratings.

## 3. Metric to measure whether the decision worked

The main metric would be Average Patient Waiting Time (in minutes).

For example:

- Before the decision: Average waiting time was 90 minutes.

- After the decision: Average waiting time reduced to 45 minutes.

This would represent a 50% reduction in average waiting time.

## Successful outcome

The decision would be considered successful if the average patient waiting time decreased without negatively affecting the quality of patient care or patient satisfaction.

## Conclusion

Data analysis helps hospitals make informed decisions rather than relying on assumptions. By analyzing patient volumes and waiting times, hospital management can allocate staff more effectively, improve patient experiences and deliver better healthcare services.

A useful way to remember this for your data analytics studies:

- Decision: What action should the business take?

- Data: What information is needed to make that decision?

- Metric: How will we know whether the decision worked?

**Example: E-commerce Industry – Reducing Shopping Cart Abandonment**

**1. Business problem and concrete decision**

An online clothing store notices that many customers add products to their shopping carts but leave the website without completing their purchases.

**Decision:** Should the business offer free delivery or a discount to encourage customers to complete their purchases?

**Data analysis can help the business understand why customers abandon their shopping carts and determine which strategy is most effective**.

**2. Data required**

The business would need to collect:
- Customer shopping cart records.
- Number of products added to carts.
- Number of completed purchases.
- Website visits and customer browsing behaviour.
- Product prices.
- Delivery fees.
- Customer payment records.
- Customer feedback about the checkout process.

**3. Metric to measure whether the decision worked**

The main metric would be **Shopping Cart Conversion Rate**.

This measures the percentage of shopping carts that result in completed purchases.

For example:

| **Description** | **Before** | **After** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shopping carts created | 1,000 | 1,000 |
| Completed purchases | 300 | 450 |
| Conversion rate | 30% | 45% |

The conversion rate increased from 30% to 45%, suggesting that the new strategy encouraged more customers to complete their purchases.

## Positive outcome

The decision would be successful if more customers complete their purchases and the additional profit exceeds the cost of offering free delivery or discounts.

**4. Conclusion**

Data analysis helps e-commerce businesses understand customer shopping behaviour and identify reasons why potential customers do not complete purchases.

By analyzing customer transactions, website activity and checkout behaviour, the business can make better decisions, improve the shopping experience and increase sales.

**Connecting it to the OSEMN analytics lifecycle**

| **Stage** | **Application** |
| --- | --- |
| Ask | Why are customers abandoning their shopping carts? |
| Obtain | Collect website, shopping cart and transaction data. |
| Scrub | Remove duplicate records and correct missing information. |
| Explore | Identify where customers leave the checkout process. |
| Model | Compare the impact of free delivery versus discounts. |
| Interpret/Communicate | Recommend the strategy that improves purchases profitably. |

**Remember:** The key difference is that the Safeway example focuses on fair pricing and customer trust, while the e-commerce example focuses on customer behaviour and converting online visits into sales.

You now have three useful industry examples: healthcare, retail and e-commerce. These are excellent examples to practice identifying a business decision, the data required and the success metric.
